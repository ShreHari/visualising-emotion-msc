Text-to-image · emotional similarity

How close is the drawing's feeling to the words'?

At a science festival, people typed a few words and an AI drew each one. This instrument runs the research pipeline live — it describes a drawing, reads the feeling from that description, then measures how close the two are. There is no correct answer to find.

  1. 1DescribeFive plain questions — expression, action, posture, people, scene. Never “what emotion is this?”
  2. 2ReadOne model infers a feeling from those answers; a different one reads your words.
  3. 3CompareMeasured against random pairings — how close, not right or wrong.

Show the measurement geometry

The similarity number is a distance. Each reading is placed on a map of feelings — left to right is unpleasant to pleasant, bottom to top is calm to excited — and the score is how close the two placements land.

This is the weakest of the four measures used in the research: it cannot tell a face apart from a scene. The two checks on the left are the ones the finding rests on.

Method and limits

Describe, don't ask

Forcing an image model to output an emotion label directly performs poorly on line art: the CLIP baseline this project measured reached 25% emotion-family agreement, against 49% for the pipeline used here. So instead one model answers plain descriptive questions, and a separate model infers a feeling from those answers.

The two sides are read by different models — GoEmotions for your words, zero-shot NLI for the drawing's description — because each suits its input. Their scores are put on the same seven-family scale before they are compared.

Why chance isn't zero

These measures do not start at nought. Take a drawing and a sentence at random from the dataset and they already score about 76% on feeling similarity, because most feelings sit near one another on the map. Worse, random pairs are spread right across the scale — so on any single comparison the similarity number is weak evidence, and the two yes/no checks matter more.

Every chance figure shown here is measured from this project's own data by shuffling the pairings 300 times — the same procedure used in the dissertation.

Similarity, not accuracy

Emotion labels overlap, so this measures closeness rather than exact match: “happy” and “excited” belong to the same family and count as similar. There is no pass mark and no correct answer.

How well any of this matches human judgement is the next study — no human ratings have been collected yet.

Faces read far better than scenes

Where the describer reports a readable face, the two readings agree on the valence sign 74% of the time (chance 54%). Where it reports a scene with no person, that falls to 43% (chance 30%). The split is the describer's, not a human's — and the describer names an expression even when it reports no person; when that invented clause is removed, the scene figure falls to chance. If you upload a picture with no one in it, treat the result as weak — the pipeline has much less to go on than the confident-looking output suggests.

The same words do not always give the same reading

87 prompts in the dataset were drawn more than once. Given identical words, the reader returns the same emotion for 69% of the drawings in a group, against 59% for randomly regrouped drawings — more consistent than chance, but far from fixed. Half the groups are pairs, so this figure cannot fall below 50%. It measures the whole prompt–image–reader chain, not the generator alone.

Known limits

The drawing-reader is biased towards happiness: it reports a happy or smiling expression for 57% of the drawings whose face it can read (66% of the dataset as a whole), which inflates positive results. Fear, disgust and surprise are poorly captured by both readers — the one reading the drawing and the one reading your words. A low score can therefore mean the two readers disagree about the drawing, or simply that one of them missed it.

Your words and any image you upload are processed in memory and never written to disk, and this server keeps no record of visitors. Names of private individuals in the festival entries have been replaced with a placeholder; every drawing is shown.
